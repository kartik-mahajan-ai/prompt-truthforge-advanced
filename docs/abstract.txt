Systems employing retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) are becoming adept at checking their responses and repairing claims that have been flagged as erroneous. However, we ask whether it is worthwhile to make such repairs. Our system, TruthForge, parses an answer into claims and verifies every claim against the retrieved evidence using a natural language inference model. For each claim it determines one of four actions: accept, rewrite, retrieve again, or abstain. We carry out our experiments on 300 responses from RAGTruth, comprising 2,089 claims of which 125 were labelled as unsupported. Correcting every claim that has been flagged removes none of the annotated errors and increases the number of tokens by 79%. TruthForge, however, removes 73 of the 125 errors, all by abstention, while its token count is 19% less than that of fixing all claims, and it withholds 272 correct claims. A human annotation of 50 rewrites shows that the precision of the verifier's judgement of correction damage is 6.7%. When applied to a dataset with unanswerable questions, TruthForge refuses 76%, while the generator refuses only 56%. The addition of an answerability gate increases this to 96%; however, this increase is not statistically significant (p = 0.063).
